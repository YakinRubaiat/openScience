# KRAS-G12C inhibitor AZ-47 suppresses tumor cell growth and induces apoptosis in KRASG12C-mutant non-small cell lung cancer models

**Working draft — generated with openScience writing agents (simulated data)**

## Abstract

KRAS is the most frequently mutated oncogene in human cancer, and the KRAS G12C allele occurs in ~13% of lung adenocarcinomas. Although covalent G12C inhibitors such as sotorasib and adagrasib have reached the clinic, acquired resistance and incomplete pathway suppression limit durable responses, and additional chemical matter with differentiated profiles remains valuable. We characterized AZ-47, a novel covalent KRAS G12C inhibitor, in preclinical models of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). We measured viability across a panel of KRASG12C-mutant (NCI-H358, NCI-H1373, NCI-H2122) and KRAS wild-type (A549) NSCLC cell lines, profiled signaling by immunoblot, and quantified apoptosis by caspase-3/7 activation and PARP cleavage. AZ-47 potently inhibits viability of KRASG12C-mutant NSCLC cell lines (IC50 4-9 nM) while sparing KRAS wild-type lines, and induces apoptosis through suppression of RAF-MEK-ERK signaling. These data nominate AZ-47 as a potent and selective preclinical KRAS G12C inhibitor and provide a framework for evaluating next-generation KRAS-targeted agents.

## Introduction

KRAS is the most frequently mutated oncogene in human cancer, with activating mutations detected in approximately 25% of all tumors. For more than three decades KRAS was considered undruggable because its smooth protein surface lacks tractable binding pockets. The discovery of a cryptic switch-II pocket in the GDP-bound state of the KRASG12C mutant enabled the first covalent inhibitors, culminating in the approval of sotorasib and adagrasib for KRASG12C-mutant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).

However, clinical responses to first-generation G12C inhibitors are often modest and transient: median progression-free survival is roughly 6-7 months, and resistance emerges through RTK reactivation, secondary KRAS mutations, and MAPK pathway bypass. Moreover, many covalent scaffolds show incomplete target engagement in vivo. Addressing this gap is significant because identifying chemically distinct inhibitors with deeper pathway suppression could improve response durability and provide back-up compounds for combination strategies.

Here, we set out to characterize the cellular pharmacology of AZ-47, a novel covalent KRAS G12C inhibitor, across a panel of NSCLC models. To this end, we profiled antiproliferative activity in three KRASG12C-mutant cell lines (NCI-H358, NCI-H1373, NCI-H2122) and the KRAS wild-type A549 line, and we examined downstream signaling and apoptosis using immunoblotting and caspase assays. We show that AZ-47 potently inhibits viability of KRASG12C-mutant NSCLC cell lines (IC50 4-9 nM) while sparing KRAS wild-type lines, and induces apoptosis through suppression of RAF-MEK-ERK signaling.

## Methods

Study design. All experiments were performed as three independent biological replicates. Dose-response studies used 10-point, 3-fold serial dilutions of AZ-47 (0.1 nM to 10 uM).

Model system. Human NSCLC cell lines NCI-H358, NCI-H1373, and NCI-H2122 (KRASG12C-mutant) and A549 (KRAS wild-type) were obtained from ATCC, authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling, and cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 C in 5% CO2. Lines were routinely tested and found negative for mycoplasma.

Compounds and reagents. AZ-47 (purity >98% by LC-MS) was synthesized in-house and dissolved in DMSO; final DMSO concentration did not exceed 0.1% (v/v). Sotorasib (AMG 510) was used as a reference inhibitor.

Assays. Cell viability was measured 72 h after compound addition using CellTiter-Glo (Promega); IC50 values were derived from four-parameter logistic fits. Signaling was assessed by immunoblotting of whole-cell lysates with antibodies against phospho-ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204), total ERK1/2, cleaved PARP, and GAPDH. Apoptosis was quantified 24 h after treatment using a Caspase-Glo 3/7 assay (Promega), normalized to cell number.

Statistical analysis. Data are presented as mean +/- SD of three biological replicates. Group differences were evaluated by two-way ANOVA with Sidak's multiple comparisons test; P < 0.05 was considered significant. Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 10.

## Results

AZ-47 selectively inhibits viability of KRASG12C-mutant NSCLC cells. Across the KRASG12C-mutant lines NCI-H358, NCI-H1373, and NCI-H2122, AZ-47 reduced viability with IC50 values of 4.2, 8.7, and 6.1 nM, respectively, whereas the KRAS wild-type A549 line was insensitive (IC50 > 5 uM), indicating a >600-fold selectivity window (Fig. 1A). (P < 0.001 for mutant vs. wild-type, two-way ANOVA)

AZ-47 suppresses RAF-MEK-ERK signaling. Immunoblot analysis of NCI-H358 cells treated for 4 h showed dose-dependent reduction of ERK1/2 phosphorylation, with near-complete suppression at 100 nM, comparable to the reference inhibitor sotorasib (Fig. 1B).

Pathway suppression by AZ-47 triggers apoptosis. Caspase-3/7 activity increased 6.8-fold after 24 h of treatment with 100 nM AZ-47, accompanied by PARP cleavage on immunoblot; no caspase activation was observed in KRAS wild-type A549 cells (Fig. 1C). (P < 0.001 vs. vehicle)

## Discussion

In this study, we show that the novel covalent inhibitor AZ-47 potently and selectively suppresses the viability of KRASG12C-mutant NSCLC cells, with single-digit nanomolar IC50 values and a wide selectivity window over KRAS wild-type cells. Suppression of phospho-ERK and induction of caspase-3/7 activity and PARP cleavage indicate that antiproliferative activity reflects on-target inhibition of KRASG12C signaling leading to apoptosis rather than nonspecific cytotoxicity.

The cellular potency of AZ-47 is comparable to that reported for sotorasib and adagrasib in the same genetic background. As with those agents, durable pathway suppression correlated with apoptotic commitment, consistent with the view that depth of initial pathway inhibition is a determinant of clinical benefit.

Mechanistically, covalent engagement of the switch-II pocket locks KRASG12C in its inactive GDP-bound state, and the subnanomolar-to-nanomolar activity observed here suggests efficient target engagement at low drug exposures.

Our study has limitations. Our experiments were confined to in vitro cell line models; in vivo pharmacokinetics and efficacy remain to be established. We did not assess acquired resistance, which frequently limits first-generation G12C inhibitors. Covalent proteomics to confirm selective engagement of KRASG12C over other cysteine-containing proteins was not performed.

Future work should evaluate AZ-47 in xenograft and patient-derived models, characterize resistance mechanisms, and explore rational combinations with RTK or SHP2 inhibitors.

In conclusion, AZ-47 is a potent and selective preclinical KRAS G12C inhibitor whose cellular profile supports further development as a candidate for KRAS-mutant lung cancer.
